## 2. My Concrete Outputs

### 2.1. Overall Game Concept and Cross-Phase Design

I developed the overall ARBIVERSE concept and integrated storyline, connecting three historical financial events into a progressive educational trading game.

- **Phase 1 – Tulip Mania:** Speculative trading, arbitrage opportunities, market sentiment, inventory exposure, and bubble-crash risk.
- **Phase 2 – Thai Baht Crisis:** Currency arbitrage, exchange-rate discrepancies, transaction costs, and trading restrictions.
- **Phase 3 – JPY Carry Trade:** Currency borrowing, FX exposure, portfolio allocation, investment returns, and debt repayment.

I worked with teammates to develop the trading rules, financial constraints, and decision-making structures across the three phases.

My focus was to maintain consistency between financial learning objectives, gameplay mechanics, calculation formulas, and scoring criteria while preserving the distinct financial challenges of each phase.

### 2.2. Phase 3 – Financial Formulas and Excel Model

I took primary responsibility for developing the financial calculation framework for Phase 3.

I built an Excel financial model covering:

- **JPY Borrowing:** Borrowing amounts, outstanding principal, and financing costs.
- **FX Transactions:** JPY/USD conversion, exchange-rate movements, and FX gains or losses.
- **Portfolio Investment:** Investment allocation, asset returns, and portfolio valuation.
- **Interest and Debt Repayment:** Interest expenses, outstanding liabilities, and repayment obligations.
- **Solvency Assessment:** Whether available assets and cash could cover outstanding debt.
- **Final Financial Performance:** Net wealth and profitability after accounting for investment returns, FX movements, and borrowing costs.

The model connected these calculations into a complete financing and investment process.

For example, a player could earn positive returns from US investments but still suffer an overall loss if the JPY appreciated significantly before debt repayment.

I used the model to simulate different player strategies and market conditions, providing quantitative reference results for implementing and validating the playable game.

### 2.3. Phase 1 & Phase 3 – Scoring Framework Development

I co-developed the Phase 1 scoring framework and took primary responsibility for developing Phase 3's scoring system.

**Phase 1 – Tulip Mania**

I contributed to designing and refining the 100-point scoring framework:

| Scoring Component             | Maximum Points |
| ----------------------------- | -------------: |
| Financial Performance         |             40 |
| Arbitrage & Trading Execution |             30 |
| Decision Quality              |             15 |
| Risk Management               |             15 |
| **Total**                     |        **100** |

I also refined the Financial Performance benchmark based on gameplay testing.

After observing that the maximum achievable net return was approximately 40%, I adjusted the full-score threshold to 35%.

This made the scoring benchmark challenging but achievable under the game's actual trading conditions.

I also contributed to scoring mechanisms intended to discourage passive gameplay and unnecessary trading, ensuring that players were rewarded for meaningful participation and sound financial decisions.

**Phase 3 – JPY Carry Trade**

I developed the Phase 3 scoring framework to evaluate:

- **Financial Performance:** FX results and portfolio investment profitability.
- **Risk Management:** Debt exposure, liquidity, and portfolio risk.
- **Decision Quality:** Borrowing decisions, capital allocation, position sizing, and repayment strategy.

The framework was designed to distinguish sound financial decisions from profitability driven mainly by favorable market movements.

A player achieving high returns through excessive borrowing should not automatically receive the same evaluation as a player who generated returns while managing financial risks effectively.

## 7. What I Personally Learned

- **Financial models must connect the entire transaction:** Building Phase 3 taught me that investment returns cannot be evaluated independently from borrowing costs, currency movements, and debt repayment. A profitable portfolio does not necessarily mean a profitable carry trade.

- **A high return does not always mean a good decision:** While developing the scoring framework, I learned that profitability alone cannot fully reflect the quality of a financial strategy. Risk exposure, liquidity management, and decision-making should also influence performance evaluation.

- **Scoring benchmarks need practical testing:** The Phase 1 scoring revision showed me that theoretical scoring thresholds may not reflect achievable gameplay results. Testing different strategies is necessary to establish realistic evaluation standards.

- **Excel modeling is valuable for game validation:** Creating an independent financial model helped me verify expected outcomes and identify inconsistencies that might not be obvious during normal gameplay.

- **Game design requires balancing realism and accessibility:** Financial markets involve complex relationships, but an educational game must simplify them without losing the main economic lessons. I learned to focus on the financial mechanisms that most directly affect player decisions.

## 8. Difficulties Encountered and How I Handled Them

**Phase 3 involved several interconnected financial calculations.**

JPY borrowing, FX conversion, US asset investment, interest expenses, and debt repayment all affected the final outcome. Calculating these components separately could produce inconsistent results.

I addressed this by building an integrated Excel model that followed the complete financing and investment process. I then tested different financial scenarios to verify the relationships among portfolio value, currency movements, and liabilities.

**The scoring framework could reward favorable market movements rather than sound decisions.**

A player could generate substantial profit by taking excessive borrowing or investment risk. Evaluating performance based only on returns would therefore provide an incomplete assessment.

I addressed this by developing separate scoring criteria for financial performance, risk management, and decision quality, allowing the game to evaluate how players made decisions as well as what they earned.

**Phase 1's financial-performance threshold needed recalibration.**

During testing, the maximum achievable net return was approximately 40%. An unsuitable full-score threshold could make the scoring system unrealistic.

I adjusted the benchmark to 35%, using observed gameplay results to make the threshold challenging but attainable.

**Testing every possible strategy was impractical.**

The combination of different borrowing amounts, exchange-rate movements, investment allocations, and repayment decisions created numerous possible outcomes.

I developed 40 Phase 3 test scenarios covering different financial conditions, including unfavorable market movements and repayment difficulties. This provided a structured basis for validating the model, although additional testing with real players would further improve scoring calibration.

## 9. Message for Future Generations

Financial simulations should teach players how financial decisions work, not simply reward them for making money.

Based on my experience developing ARBIVERSE, I would share the following advice:

1. **Start with the financial concept before designing the game.** Identify what players should learn and build trading mechanics around that objective.

2. **Build an Excel model before implementing complex financial calculations.** A reference model makes it easier to verify formulas and detect inconsistencies when the playable game is developed.

3. **Never evaluate trading performance based on profit alone.** Include decision quality and risk management so the scoring framework rewards financially sound strategies rather than favorable market movements.

4. **Test the scoring framework with realistic scenarios.** A scoring formula may look reasonable but still create unrealistic benchmarks or unintended incentives. Use simulation results to adjust thresholds.

5. **Include unfavorable market conditions in your testing.** Currency appreciation, investment losses, insufficient liquidity, and repayment difficulties are essential for checking whether a financial model captures real risk.

6. **Keep financial formulas and game rules consistent across the team.** When different members develop mechanics, calculations, and scoring, shared definitions and clear documentation help prevent contradictions.

7. **Treat testing as part of development, not the final step.** Every major change to a financial formula or scoring rule should be followed by another round of validation.

8. **Document both successful results and necessary revisions.** A corrected formula or recalibrated benchmark demonstrates how testing improves the product and provides meaningful evidence of individual contribution.

The most important lesson I learned is that **a successful financial game should help players understand why they make or lose money, how financial risks interact, and what distinguishes good financial decisions from lucky outcomes.**
